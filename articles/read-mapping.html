Read Mapping

Bioinformatic Pipelines Explained

By Noah Legall

The fundamental activity of bioinformatics - the most routine of analyses one can do as a bioinformatician - is SNP calling. For the uninitiated, SNP calling is the process of taking raw sequencing data from multiple genome isolates and generating, as output, summaries of the nucleotide differences between those isolates. When considering bacterial pathogens and their ability to colonize different hosts or environments, having this level of nucleotide resolution between isolates can help elucidate mutations that confer a potential selective advantage on the bacteria.

Personally, I’ve made a career out of doing this over and over again. In my PhD, I used SNPs found in a wildlife pathogen to discern which mutations led to colonization within different host species and geographic locations. For The Microbialist, my research partner Aspen and I developed a pipeline to call SNPs from Mycobacterium tuberculosis isolates that were resistant to different drugs. It’s the basic skill most bioinformaticians should know - and if you can learn how to generate mutation data from isolates, you can use that data as input for more complex, tertiary analyses involving machine learning, epidemiology, and, increasingly, LLM-focused applications.

Nowadays, applying SNP calling workflows is pretty much instantaneous. There is little to no friction in taking raw sequencing data and finding all of the mutations within that dataset. However, I refuse to believe that the ease of applying this skill should mean we as a community retreat from trying to explain this workflow in more clearer, creative, and engaging ways.

The issue I notice, though, is that tutorials have not really adapted to this new landscape. The truth is that even with the power A.I. can provide for learning the key details of workflows and algorithms, the majority of learning materials are still stuck in a sort of format-hibernation.

I’d like to demonstrate what I mean by exploring one of the easiest topics every bioinformatician can grasp - read mapping. In revisiting a topic so many of us know intimately, we can see how this format can be enhanced to shed light on how to write and discuss ideas in more interactive ways.

Shredding the Genome to obtain ‘reads’

Every living thing has the instructions for its functions encoded in DNA. And organisms have a lot of instructions to encode. For bacteria, the entire repertoire of functions is encoded in genomes that are measured in millions of nucleotides. Being able to read the DNA found within an organism of interest could provide valuable insights into how certain phenotypes develop. Anyone who’s been following The Microbialist knows that we have investigated the link between DNA sequences and antibiotic resistance - the major hypothesis at the basis of this work is that these differences between bacterial responses are primarily captured at the genomic level (it might not even be an assumption most people make, but it is an assumption nonetheless). Thus, it makes sense that we have a way of recording the genome as it is, so we can decipher the secrets and insights it might hold.

For a long time, though, the obstacle was that the genome as it was structured within cells was not easily recordable. There was no instrument or technique that could isolate the genome as-is and provide the exact sequence information to a scientist. There were ways of identifying the DNA sequence of particular regions through sequencing, but these methods were not designed to be high-throughput - meaning that the amount of data generated from these methods was typically smaller and more specific.

There is a version of this post that describes the history of sequencing technologies, but we will circumvent the topic for now. What ultimately is important to recognize is that sequencing the genome through modern whole-genome sequencing requires that the entire genome is fragmented (‘shredded’) into short sequences that can be sequenced in parallel by modern sequencing platforms, such as those created by the company Illumina.

The output of this process is known as a sequencing read - bioinformaticians typically refer to these as just ‘reads’. How we utilize reads to assess the genome is the interesting part I’d like to cover in this post, and we will delve into the intricacies through interactive examples.

Read Mapping as a puzzle

Reads are delivered to the bioinformatician in a file format known as a FASTQ file. There are many details to FASTQ files, but for our exercise today, let’s simplify and state that a FASTQ is a catalogue of the reads recovered from the sequencing process. The issue is that, from these small reads, the bioinformatician is tasked with recasting the genome. This is analogous to solving a Mona Lisa puzzle from smaller fragments of the painting.

We are in luck, however! The process of recreating the genome is made easier by the fact that we have example reference genomes to compare against and see where a read could come from. So, to keep the analogy alive, if you are given a puzzle set, it would be easier to assemble if you knew at the outset that you were recreating the Mona Lisa AND you had an outline of where to put the pieces!

Reads are mapped through the use of terrific and sophisticated algorithms, but I’d like us to get an intuitive sense of what we are trying to do with the short reads. Some things to note here: this example is the most ‘golden path’ example I could devise to dip our feet into the world of read mapping. I designed this activity so that each read is a perfect subset of the original sequence, which guarantees that there is a perfect match to align the read to.

Naturally, the interesting things about a field rarely exist on the golden path - it’s time to go off-road! Let’s now have a bit more fun with the scenario by relaxing a few of the constraints we made for this toy example.

A SNP as a needle in the haystack

The first assumption we will flex here is that reads are perfect representations of the reference. Sometimes the organism being sequenced possesses a mutation at a particular position compared to the reference genome we are given. As the reader, you might have noticed that certain reads overlap at a particular region more so than others. This is entirely random, but several reads could overlap a region in the reference.

This overlap can help us solidify that we confidently placed a read at the right position - if the nucleotide on the read matches the reference nucleotide at that position, then we are on the right track in deciphering the genome. On the other hand, if every overlapping read differs from the reference at a position, this might present strong evidence of a mutation in the isolate we are analyzing. This is essentially how a single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) is identified: based on the agreement of overlapping reads at that position. Let’s take a look:

Side note: I wanted to highlight the potential SNP region to solidify the point of nucleotides in reads disagreeing with the reference. At the onset of read mapping, there is no way to know where the SNP will be.

Is this a mistake or an actual mutation?

Okay, neat. So we know that the overlap of reads can help in elucidating potential SNP sites versus agreement with the reference. One consideration to take note of, however, is that not all discrepancies found in the reads are indicative of a mutation - some are just errors inherent to the sequencing process. Typically, sequencers make small mistakes in the reads such that a nucleotide is inaccurate at a certain position, preventing the read from being an accurate representation of the genome it was sequenced from.

These reads will disagree with the best placement, similar to reads that encompass a mutation. So we now have to be guarded about how we define what is a SNP versus what is a sequencing error. As if our jobs weren’t hard enough! However, we are not without techniques to decipher between the two scenarios. Before I tell you a strategy to know the difference, take a moment to look at another simulated read mapping, and ask yourself what seems like a good strategy that could be exploited:

Matching base Mismatch (a sequencing error, or the SNP when it falls in the highlighted column) Potential SNP (reference base; reads covering it carry a different base)

The keen-eyed reader might notice that at our SNP site, there is massive agreement amongst the overlapping reads for that mutation. For one-off sequencing errors, however, this was not the case. For overlapping reads, we found that at an error position, only one read had a nucleotide that differed from the other reads at that position.

Based on that insight, we can assess what is a true SNP versus an error based on the agreement of the overlapping reads. If only a small subset of reads in the overlap have an alternative nucleotide, then we can attribute the discrepancy to an error. This is why bioinformaticians care about metrics like coverage at the site of a mutation. The higher the coverage at a nucleotide, the more confident we can be in identifying a mutation! Higher coverage means a larger overlap at a particular position. If we see a mutation suggested in a region with consensus at the overlap, we know that this isn’t simply chance! Exciting!

Tricky placement of reads in redundant regions

Okay, we know how to find SNPs, and we can allow for a few errors or mismatches in the reads without the entire premise of the platform falling apart.

Naturally, if the reads can have some assumptions broken, our reference can be challenged too, so we can better understand the challenges in read mapping. Let’s relax the assumption that the reference has no repeating regions in the sequence.

Let’s redo the read mapping exercise, but this time, take note of anything that appears to be challenging:

Matching base Mismatch (a sequencing error, or the SNP when it falls in the highlighted column) Potential SNP (reference base; reads covering it carry a different base) Redundant region (the same 18 bases appear twice in the reference)

How was this different from our previous example? You might have experienced a challenge in deciding where to place a read - multiple regions in the reference could have accepted it. Right now it seems innocuous enough, but if we are unable to differentiate where to place a read that has a potential mutation, then we lose the detail about spatial effects that the SNP can provide. In the example simulation I generated, our inability to know where the read could be mapped directly interferes with our process of assessing SNP positions. Essentially, the read is then useless. SAD!

This scenario is more commonplace than one would expect, especially when researchers analyze actual genomic data. I would argue this is the predominant obstacle in read mapping for finding mutations within genomes. I mean, genomes are complex, sure, but surprisingly redundant. For the bacterial genome I have experience with, Mycobacterium tuberculosis, at least 10% of the genome’s coding regions encode pathogenic features that contain redundant nucleotides. It was common in analysis that, due to the challenges in resolving where to map the reads, these reads would just be expunged from the analysis!

Side note: usually a read mapping algorithm will report a mapping quality (MQ) for each read. MQ is essentially a measure of how uniquely the read can map. The higher the MQ, the more confident we can be that the read fits in its exact place on the reference.

Make the reads longer!

The trouble with trying to resolve redundant regions in the genome is that the redundant section is often bigger than the size of the reads that were sequenced. This is the limitation of short-read sequencing technology: the reads are often not long enough to fully span the most redundant regions.

To address this limitation, multiple companies are sprouting up that use new modalities to create reads that are much longer than the standard short reads most researchers have experience with. These reads can reach 1,000 - 100,000 base pairs in length! Let’s see how longer reads can make it easier to recreate the genome and find mutations:

Matching base Mismatch (a sequencing error, or the SNP when it falls in the highlighted column) Potential SNP (reference base; reads covering it carry a different base) Redundant region (the same 18 bases appear twice in the reference)

Of course, there is still a balance to strike with the advantages that technology like this provides. You might have noticed that there were many mismatches along the longer read. This is common with this type of method, and it is typically a deterrent to wider adoption. Hypothetically, the error rate can be balanced out with greater coverage at a base, especially if the errors are randomly introduced. However, more coverage will largely mean more cost - an issue for cash-strapped labs. While this technology is exciting, and a personal favorite of mine, these limitations are obstacles the methodology needs to overcome before widespread adoption can take hold.

Conclusions

What we worked on here is a jumpstart in how bioinformaticians discuss some of the questions and methods that they encounter everyday. How reads map, or the methods used to overcome real obstacles are useful in troubleshooting how we assess the data we receive. The wild thing here - most algorithms can be discusses in terms of this visual framework. In later posts I’m looking forward to expanding this framework to discuss even more details related to how we analyze reads - the wheels in my head are already thinking of ways of expanding this to describe things like the algorithm for BWA and BLAST, or even my personal favorite algorithm for De Bruijin Graphs.

I’m actually quite interested to hear how people receive this type of article. The truth is, knowing how read mapping works in practice is not necessarily a make-or-break skill. But my hope is that by reinvigorating the format of understanding this crucial process, core understanding of key bioinformatic processes will not atrophy.

It’s a start at least.
